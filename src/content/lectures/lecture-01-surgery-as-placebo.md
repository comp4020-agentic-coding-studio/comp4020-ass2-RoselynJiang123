## Summary

In 1959 and 1960, two small trials compared an operation for angina with a skin incision alone. The incision did about as well. This lecture starts there and asks why the method those trials used — performing an operation that does nothing, on purpose — stayed rare in surgery for the next forty years, while every new drug had to beat a placebo to reach the market.

## Outline

1. **The internal mammary artery ligation trials, 1959–60.** What was done to each arm, what patients were told, and how quickly the operation disappeared afterwards.
2. **Beecher's argument.** Surgery produces placebo effects at least as large as drugs, and the surgeon's enthusiasm is one of their causes.
3. **How surgery acquired its evidence.** Case series, apprenticeship, the mechanism that sounds right, and the operation that is taught before it is tested.
4. **Wartolowska's count.** In most placebo-controlled surgical trials the placebo arm improved too, and in many the difference between arms was small or absent.
5. **What this course is for.** Twelve weeks on how to build the comparison that surgery has mostly done without.
