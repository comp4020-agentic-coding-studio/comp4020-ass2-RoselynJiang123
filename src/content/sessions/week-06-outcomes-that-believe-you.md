## The claim

Pain, function and "feeling better" are what patients want from an operation. They are also the outcomes most responsive to belief. That is not an argument against measuring them. It is the reason for the sham.

Objective measures are not a hiding place. Exercise time depends on how hard a patient is willing to push. Blood pressure moves with attention and with how and when it is measured. An outcome is not safe from expectation because it has units.

The choice of outcome, and of when to measure it, is where a sham earns or loses its cost. Measure too early and you capture recovery from the procedure itself. Measure too late and you lose patients, and the question.

## Before the Round

Compare the primary outcomes of **Moseley 2002**, **ORBITA** and **SYMPLICITY HTN-3**. For each, note:

- what was measured, and who measured it;
- the timepoint, and what might have happened after it;
- whether the outcome depends on the patient's effort or report.

## In the room

**Defend your timepoint.** Each person states their primary outcome and timepoint. Their table has been told to disagree.

Then the sample size. You will be given a minimal clinically important difference and a standard deviation for your outcome. The worked example below uses a pain scale.

> **Worked example.** Outcome: pain on a 0–10 scale. Minimal clinically important difference: 1.5 points. Standard deviation: 2.5. Two-sided α = 0.05, power 90%.
> n per arm ≈ 2 × (1.96 + 1.28)² × 2.5² ÷ 1.5² ≈ 59.
> Allowing 15% loss to follow-up: 59 ÷ 0.85 ≈ 70 per arm, 140 in total.

## Key terms

- **Primary outcome.** The single outcome the trial is powered for and judged on.
- **Minimal clinically important difference (MCID).** The smallest change patients would regard as worthwhile.
- **Timepoint.** When the primary outcome is measured. Chosen before the trial starts.
- **Power.** The probability that the trial detects a real difference of the stated size.
