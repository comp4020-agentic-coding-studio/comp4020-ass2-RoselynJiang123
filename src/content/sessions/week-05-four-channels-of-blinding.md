## The claim

Blinding is not one switch. It is four. A trial can blind the **patient**, the **operator**, the **outcome assessor** and the **analyst**. In surgical trials the operator can almost never be blinded: someone has to know whether to do the operation.

So the real design problem is containment. The operator's knowledge has to be stopped from leaking into the other three channels. A rung on the Ladder protects only the patient's channel. The rest is logistics: who is in the room, who writes the notes, who makes the follow-up call, and what they are allowed to say.

## Before the Round

About ninety minutes.

- **Schulz KF & Grimes DA, Lancet 2002**, on blinding. Read for the argument that "double-blind" is too vague to be useful, and what to say instead.
- **ORBITA methods.** Read the section on sedation and auditory isolation. Note what problem the headphones were solving.
- **Bang H et al., Control Clin Trials 2004.** Read for the idea, not the algebra: patients and assessors are asked to guess the allocation, and the pattern of guesses is summarised as an index. An index near zero means guessing is no better than chance.

## In the room

**Leak audit.** Follow one fictional patient, Mr P, from consent to twelve-month follow-up. Mark every point where his allocation could leak, and to whom.

| Point in the pathway | Who could learn the allocation | How it could leak | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Theatre list | Ward nurses, bed managers | Different procedure codes or booked durations | Identical listing and booked time |
| In theatre | The patient, if awake | Sounds, instructions, duration | Sedation, headphones, scripted team |
| Recovery | Recovery nurses, then the patient | Different analgesia, different observations | One recovery protocol for both arms |
| Dressing and wound | The patient, family, GP | Size, number or position of wounds | Matched dressings (L5) |
| Discharge letter | GP, patient | Operation note copied into the letter | Trial-specific letter without the operation note |
| Two-week phone call | The caller, the patient | The caller knows the arm and sounds like it | Caller from the blinded team only |
| Outcome assessment | Assessor | The patient mentions what they remember | Script and a "please do not tell me" instruction |
| Analysis | Analyst | Arms labelled by name | Arms coded A and B until the analysis is locked |

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0:00 | Tables walk the pathway and fill the table for their own procedure. |
| 0:45 | Swap tables. Each table tries to find one leak the other table missed. |
| 1:10 | Decide when you will ask patients and assessors to guess the allocation, and what you will do with the answers. |

## Key terms

- **Allocation concealment.** Hiding the upcoming allocation before a patient is randomised. Different from blinding, which hides it afterwards.
- **Blinding channel.** One of the four groups who could know the allocation.
- **Unblinding.** Any event that lets someone in a blinded channel learn the allocation.
- **Blinding index.** A summary of how well patients or assessors can guess their allocation.

## Protocol log — §5 Blinding plan

State who is blind, who cannot be, and how you stop the second group telling the first. Include your leak audit table and when you will collect allocation guesses. Up to 400 words plus the table.

## Check yourself

1. Why does ORBITA's use of headphones protect a different channel from its use of sedation?
2. Which channel is hardest to protect in your own design?
3. If most patients guess their allocation correctly, what does that tell you about the result?
