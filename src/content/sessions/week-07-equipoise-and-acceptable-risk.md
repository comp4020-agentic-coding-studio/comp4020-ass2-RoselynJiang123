## The claim

To a methodologist, the Ladder climbs towards better evidence. To an ethics committee, it climbs towards more risk, offered to someone who, by design, cannot benefit from it. Both readings are correct. This Round teaches you to read the Ladder upside down.

A sham trial can be justified when three things are true together. There is real uncertainty about whether the procedure works. The answer is worth having — for future patients, for the health system, for the patients already receiving the procedure. And the risk added by the chosen rung is as small as the question allows. Remove any one of the three and the trial cannot be defended.

The patients in the sham arm are not volunteers for nothing. They are volunteers for knowledge, and the protocol owes them an account of exactly what they are giving.

## Before the Round

About two hours. Read the three papers in this order.

- **Macklin R, NEJM 1999** — the case against placebo surgery. Read it as the strongest version of the objection you will have to answer.
- **Freeman TB et al., NEJM 1999** — the case for, written by investigators planning a sham-controlled trial of cell transplantation for Parkinson's disease.
- **Horng S & Miller FG, NEJM 2002** — the conditions under which placebo surgery can be acceptable.

Then reread the sham arm of **Freed et al. (2001)**: burr holes that did not pass through the skull. Ask what each extra millimetre would have bought.

## In the room

**The committee sits.** The class reviews three anonymised proposals. Each must be approved, approved with conditions, or declined. Minutes are taken.

| Proposal | Procedure (fictional) | Rung | The sham |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | An injection into a painful tendon sheath | L2 | Skin puncture under local anaesthetic; no injection |
| B | A catheter procedure for a heart rhythm symptom | L4 | Catheter to the heart under sedation; no ablation; matched duration and laboratory sounds |
| C | A spinal procedure for chronic back pain | L5 | General anaesthetic, matched incisions and dressings, scripted recovery |

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0:00 | Committee roles: chair, methodologist, surgeon, lay member, patient advocate. Roles rotate for each proposal. |
| 0:10 | Proposal A. Fifteen minutes. The minute-taker records the decision and the one reason that decided it. |
| 0:30 | Proposal B. |
| 0:50 | Proposal C. |
| 1:10 | The room compares minutes. Did the committee decide by rung, by risk, or by the value of the question? |

## The risk ledger

| Rung | Risk it adds over the rung below (examples) |
|---|---|
| L1 | Time, anxiety, the procedure-room environment |
| L2 | Local anaesthetic, a wound, infection, a scar |
| L3 | Instruments near structures that can be damaged; radiation for image-guided access |
| L4 | Longer time under the procedure; more sedation |
| L5 | General anaesthesia and its complications |

## Key terms

- **Equipoise.** Genuine uncertainty, within the expert community, about which arm is better.
- **Risk–knowledge ratio.** This course's shorthand for weighing what the sham arm risks against what the trial can learn.
- **Deception.** Withholding the allocation from a patient who has agreed in advance not to know it. Consent makes it permissible; Week 8 is about how.

## Protocol log — §7 Risk justification

State your rung, list the risks it adds over the rung below it, and argue why the answer is worth them. Address the strongest objection in Macklin (1999) directly. Up to 400 words.

## Check yourself

1. Why is equipoise about the expert community, not about the individual surgeon?
2. Which proposal did your committee find hardest, and why?
3. Could your own trial answer its question one rung lower?
