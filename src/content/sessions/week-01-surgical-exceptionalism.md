## The claim

Drugs have had to beat a placebo for most of a century. Surgery entered evidence-based medicine by a side door: precedent, the surgeon's judgment, and the fact that it is hard to argue with a scar. A new drug arrives with a comparator built in. A new operation usually arrives with a case series, a good outcome in the surgeon's own hands, and a mechanism that sounds right.

In 1959 a popular operation for angina — ligating the internal mammary arteries — was tested against a skin incision alone. The patients who received only the incision did about as well as the patients who received the operation. The operation was abandoned within a few years. The method that had exposed it was not taken up for decades.

This course calls that gap **surgical exceptionalism**: the working assumption that an operation, unlike a pill, does not need to be compared with its own absence. The assumption is rarely stated. It shows up instead in what gets compared with what.

## Before the Round

About ninety minutes of reading.

- **Cobb LA et al., NEJM 1959.** Read for what the control patients had done to them, and what they were told. Note how short the paper is.
- **Beecher HK, "Surgery as placebo", JAMA 1961.** Read for the argument that the surgeon's enthusiasm is a variable, not a background condition.
- **Wartolowska K et al., BMJ 2014.** A systematic review of placebo-controlled trials in surgery. Read for the headline proportions. You will cite them all semester.

## Key terms

- **Sham procedure.** A procedure that reproduces the experience of an operation without its presumed active element. Also called placebo surgery.
- **Comparator.** Whatever the treated group is measured against. The comparator decides what a trial can conclude.
- **Surgical exceptionalism.** This course's name for the assumption that operations need not be tested against their own absence.
- **Mechanism.** The account of why a procedure should work. A plausible mechanism is a reason to run a trial, not a substitute for one.

## Protocol log — §1 The procedure and its claim

Name the procedure you will design a sham for, and write the claim it makes in one sentence a patient could repeat back to you. Then write up to 150 words on why that claim has not been tested against a sham.

Your procedure must be in current use, invasive, and, as far as you can find, without a published sham-controlled trial. You will check that properly in Week 3.

- **A strong §1** names a population, a procedure, an outcome and a time: *in adults with a named condition, the procedure reduces a named symptom at a named point after surgery.*
- **A weak §1** says the procedure "helps" or "improves outcomes". Nobody can design a sham against "helps".

## Check yourself

1. What did the control patients in the 1959 trial experience that the treated patients also experienced?
2. Name one reason a surgeon might sincerely believe an operation works when it does not.
3. Why is "it has a plausible mechanism" not evidence that it works?
