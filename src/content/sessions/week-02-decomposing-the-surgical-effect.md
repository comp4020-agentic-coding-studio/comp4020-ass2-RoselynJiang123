## The claim

Improvement after an operation is a sum. The knife is credited with the whole of it.

Some conditions get better on their own: that is **natural history**. People seek treatment when they feel worst, so their next measurement is likely to be better whatever happens: that is **regression to the mean**. Patients who expect to improve report improvement. The ritual of care — the consultation, the admission, the gown, the attention — has effects of its own. Operations bring **co-interventions** with them: rest, time off work, physiotherapy, analgesia. And the person asking "how is your pain?" at follow-up is often the person who operated, and is hoping for a particular answer.

None of this is fraud. All of it is real improvement. It simply is not the operation. A sham exists to hold every one of these constant, so that whatever difference remains can be credited to the part of the procedure the sham leaves out.

## Before the Round

About seventy-five minutes of reading.

- **Barnett AG et al., Int J Epidemiol 2005**, on regression to the mean. Read for the diagram. Then try to explain it to someone who has not read it.
- **Kaptchuk TJ et al., BMJ 2006**, in which a sham device outperformed an inert pill. Read for what this implies about the "dose" of ritual: a more elaborate placebo can do more.

## In the room

**The Improvement Budget.** You are given a fictional case series: 40 patients with knee pain have an operation. Mean pain on a 0–10 scale falls from 7.1 before surgery to 3.4 at six weeks. That is 3.7 points to explain.

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0:00 | In tables, allocate the 3.7 points across the causes below. You must spend all of it. Write one sentence justifying each allocation. |
| 0:35 | Tables post their budgets on the wall. The room compares what each table left for the operation. |
| 0:55 | For each cause, decide whether a sham could hold it constant, partly hold it constant, or not touch it. |
| 1:20 | Start §2. |

| Cause | What it looks like | Can a sham hold it constant? |
|---|---|---|
| Natural history | The condition resolves or fluctuates without treatment | Yes, if both arms are followed for the same time |
| Regression to the mean | Patients enrol at their worst | Yes |
| Expectation | "I had the operation, so I should feel better" | Yes, if the patient cannot tell which arm they are in |
| Ritual of care | Admission, attention, the theatre itself | Partly — depends on the rung (Week 4) |
| Co-interventions | Rest, physiotherapy, analgesia afterwards | Only if the protocol prescribes them equally |
| Assessor hope | The surgeon asks the questions | Only with a blinded assessor (Week 5) |

There is no correct budget. There are budgets that can be defended and budgets that cannot.

## Key terms

- **Natural history.** What a condition does without treatment.
- **Regression to the mean.** The tendency of extreme measurements to be followed by less extreme ones, for statistical reasons alone.
- **Expectation effect.** Improvement caused by believing a treatment has been received.
- **Co-intervention.** Anything else that happens to patients because they had the procedure.
- **Non-specific effects.** The collective name for everything on this list.

## Protocol log — §2 The threats list

List every non-specific effect your sham must hold constant for your procedure, ranked by how much of the improvement you think it explains. For each one, write one line on how your design will hold it constant, or why it cannot. Up to 300 words.

- **A strong §2** is specific to the procedure: it knows which co-interventions this operation brings with it.
- **A weak §2** copies the table above.

## Check yourself

1. Why would regression to the mean make an ineffective operation look effective in a before-and-after study?
2. Which item on the list does a sham control least well?
3. If your budget left nothing for the operation, what would you conclude — and what would you not be entitled to conclude?
